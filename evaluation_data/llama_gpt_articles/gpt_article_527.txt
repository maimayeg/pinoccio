Books in, screens out: some Finnish pupils go back to paper after tech push

**Books In, Screens Out: Some Finnish Pupils Go Back to Paper After Tech Push**

In recent months, educators and parents in Finland have begun to reassess the role of technology in classrooms, leading to a noticeable shift back towards traditional paper-based learning methods. The movement, which has emerged amidst concerns about digital overload among students, has sparked discussions about the balancing act between technology and cognitive development.

A 2023 study conducted by the Finnish National Agency for Education indicates that nearly 30% of teachers reported an increase in student distraction when using digital devices for learning. Many educators noted that students often found it challenging to concentrate during lessons when they were allowed to use laptops and tablets. This phenomenon has prompted a re-evaluation of the extensive push for technology integration in schools, which has been prevalent in the past decade.

In Kainuu, a region that has implemented a pilot program focusing on traditional teaching methods, teachers have observed positive changes in student engagement. According to principal Marja Laitinen, “We have seen a marked improvement in students’ ability to stay focused during lessons, and their grades have shown a slight increase.” The pilot program, which reinstates the use of textbooks and paper-based assignments, has attracted support from both teachers and parents who feel that face-to-face interactions and tactile learning experiences are beneficial for students' development.

Support for this shift in approach can also be found in reports from educational research organizations. A 2022 report from the Finnish Institute of Educational Research highlighted potential drawbacks of excessive screen time. The report suggested that while technology can enhance learning, it is essential to maintain a balanced approach, as too much screen-based activity may inflate anxiety levels and decrease overall well-being among students.

While the trend towards paper-based education does echo sentiments from advocates who prioritize traditional learning methods, concerns about access to modern skills remain. Some educators caution that completely reverting to paper could hinder students' technological proficiency necessary for future workplaces. “We must strike a balance,” says Tuomas Järvinen, an educational technology analyst. “Digital literacy is vital, but we cannot ignore the need for critical thinking and reflection that comes from engaging with physical texts.”

As discussions continue about the optimal educational environment for children, Finnish schools may very well find a “middle ground” that integrates both paper-based learning and technology. While the debate unfolds, the experiences in Kainuu are being closely monitored as a potential roadmap for future educational practices across the country.
