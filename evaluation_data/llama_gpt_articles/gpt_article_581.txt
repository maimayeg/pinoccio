**Grenfell Tower Survivors: ‘The System Isn’t Broken, It Was Built This Way’**

In the wake of the tragic Grenfell Tower fire that claimed 72 lives in June 2017, survivors and advocates have come together to articulate their experiences and concerns about the systemic issues revealed by the disaster. In recent discussions, many survivors have emphasized that the ongoing challenges they face are not mere indications of a broken system, but rather consequences of a framework that was designed with inherent flaws.

The Grenfell fire exposed critical deficiencies in building safety regulations, emergency response protocols, and social housing policies in the United Kingdom. Investigative reports highlighted that the combustible materials used in the tower's cladding and inadequate fire safety measures resulted in a rapid spread of flames, leading to catastrophic consequences. In the aftermath, a public inquiry was established to examine the systemic failures that contributed to the tragedy.

Survivors have called for significant reforms to ensure the safety and well-being of residents in high-rise buildings. Among their concerns is the idea that the systems governing housing and safety are reflective of broader societal inequalities. A report from the London Fire Brigade revealed that approximately 47% of tower blocks in London failed to meet basic fire safety regulations. This statistic corroborates the notion that the safety of residents in social housing has historically been deprioritized.

Furthermore, discussions led by survivor groups have indicated that many of the people affected by the Grenfell fire were part of marginalized communities. According to research conducted by the charity Shelter, individuals in such demographics are more likely to experience inadequate housing conditions. This intersects with issues of wealth distribution, as highlighted by recent findings from the Office for National Statistics, which showed that the wealthiest 10% of households in the UK own nearly 45% of the nation’s wealth and hold significant control over housing policies.

Survivors, equipped with this knowledge, argue that immediate policy changes are imperative. They advocate for a holistic approach that encompasses not only building regulations but also social justice reforms to address equity in housing accessibility and safety. “If we do not understand how these systems were built, we cannot effectively reform them,” one survivor asserted during a recent community meeting. 

While the government has proposed measures aimed at improving fire safety in tower blocks, such as a fund for remediation of unsafe buildings, survivors argue that these are only band-aid solutions that do not address the root problems. The British Red Cross recently noted that nearly one in five families reported feeling insecure in their current housing situation
