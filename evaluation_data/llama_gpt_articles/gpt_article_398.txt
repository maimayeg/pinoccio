**As Peru Buries Fujimori, a Complex Tussle Over His Legacy**

Peru laid to rest former president Alberto Fujimori on a quiet afternoon, closing a chapter that spanned decades of political turmoil, economic transformation, and a deeply divided national sentiment. Fujimori, who passed away at the age of 85, was a figure both revered and reviled, embodying the complexities of Peru's journey through the late 20th century.

During his presidency from 1990 to 2000, Fujimori implemented sweeping economic reforms that included aggressive neoliberal policies aimed at stabilizing a hyperinflation-stricken economy. According to the World Bank, inflation dropped from an annual rate of 7,650% in 1990 to below 5% by 1994. His administration is credited with significant advancements in reducing poverty rates—from approximately 56% in 1990 to around 38% by the end of his term in 2000.

However, Fujimori's legacy is marred by serious human rights violations. His government was responsible for numerous extrajudicial killings and forced disappearances linked to the fight against the Shining Path terrorist group. Human Rights Watch estimates that around 69,000 people were killed during the internal conflict, many attributed to state actions. These statistics have fueled ongoing debates about his legacy, partitioning public opinion into starkly opposing camps.

As the nation mourns, many Peruvians reflect on their complex feelings about Fujimori, navigating a landscape marked by the duality of economic achievement and moral culpability. Current president Dina Boluarte, who assumed office amid significant political turmoil, has indicated a desire to foster unity and healing in a nation still reeling from Fujimori’s divisive era. However, the deep schisms regarding his governance continue to influence contemporary political discourse.

In the aftermath of his funeral, various political factions are expected to engage in discussions about how to interpret and memorialize Fujimori's time in office. Scholars from multiple disciplines, including political science and history, are contributing to these conversations, emphasizing the importance of addressing both the positive and negative consequences of his policies. 

Statistics indicate that public opinion remains divided, with various polls showing about 40% of Peruvians viewing him favorably compared to around 35% who express negative sentiments. This division poses implications for future political strategies and electoral processes as parties navigate the abstract ideals tied to Fujimori’s rule.
