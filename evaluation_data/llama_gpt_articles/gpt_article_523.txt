**Big Pharma Lacks Motive to Prep for New Pandemics**

In recent discussions surrounding global health preparedness, critics have highlighted a perceived disconnect between the pharmaceutical industry and the need for proactive pandemic readiness. Despite experiencing a seismic shift in focus during the COVID-19 crisis, many believe that the pharmaceutical sector lacks a substantial incentive to continue preparing for future pandemics.

The COVID-19 pandemic brought unprecedented attention to vaccine research and development, dramatically accelerating timelines for vaccine approval and distribution. According to a report from the World Health Organization (WHO), over 13 billion COVID-19 vaccine doses were administered worldwide, significantly shaping how both governments and companies perceive public health needs. However, as the world begins to return to a semblance of normalcy, experts suggest that Big Pharma is returning to its pre-pandemic focus on more profitable chronic disease treatments rather than preventative measures for potential pandemics.

The financial dynamics of the pharmaceutical industry present an intriguing paradox. While the pandemic showcased the potential for rapid development of lifesaving vaccines, the average timeline for bringing a new drug to market remains between 10 to 15 years, with an accompanying cost that can exceed $2.6 billion. According to industry analysts, drugs targeting non-communicable diseases typically yield higher financial returns, incentivizing companies to allocate resources toward conditions like diabetes or hypertension over potential pandemic threats that may or may not materialize.

Moreover, the frequency of pandemic threats poses a challenge in itself. The WHO notes that the emergence of new pathogens capable of causing pandemics has increased, but historically, significant pandemics occur infrequently. This can lead to a rationalization within the industry: why invest heavily in preparation for an uncertain event when immediate, high-demand markets exist for established treatments? 

Some industry observers argue that a lack of long-term contracts for potential vaccines contributes to this hesitance. “When governments do not commit to purchasing large quantities of vaccines for future pandemics, it diminishes financial motivation for the pharmaceutical companies to invest in the preparation of such products,” said Dr. Emily Russo, a public health expert.

On a global scale, the situation warrants attention. The Global Preparedness Monitoring Board cautioned that the world remains underprepared for the next pandemic, emphasizing that improved collaboration between governments, global organizations, and pharmaceutical companies is essential for effective response planning. However, as the urgency of the COVID-19 pandemic fades, there is a risk that the momentum for such collaborative efforts may also wane.
