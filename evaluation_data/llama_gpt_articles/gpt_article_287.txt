**If we can't speak, why live? - BBC meets women after new Taliban law**

In a significant shift in policy, the Taliban government has recently imposed restrictions that curtail women's ability to engage in public discourse. Under the new regulations, women are largely prohibited from participating in public speaking events, including discussions, seminars, and educational forums. The ruling has drawn attention from various humanitarian organizations, raising concerns about freedom of expression and women's rights in Afghanistan.

BBC reporters conducted interviews with several women affected by this law, highlighting the emotional and societal ramifications. One woman, who requested anonymity, stated, “If we can’t speak, why live? Our thoughts and ideas are essential for a flourishing society.” This sentiment echoes a broader concern among many women who feel increasingly marginalized in a landscape where their voices are systematically silenced.

The new restrictions align with the Taliban’s historical stance on gender roles and public life. Statistics indicate a troubling trend, with reports showing that women’s rights have diminished significantly since the Taliban regained control of Afghanistan in 2021. Gender Equality Index scores for the country have dropped, reflecting a substantial reduction in women's participation in various sectors, including education and governance. According to recent data, only 14% of Afghan women are currently active in the workforce, a dramatic decrease from previous years before the regime change.

While the Taliban has justified the enactment of these laws as aligned with Islamic principles, criticism from the international community continues to grow. Advocacy groups have emphasized the importance of women’s voices in shaping healthy communities. The United Nations has noted that societies benefit from diverse perspectives, particularly those of women, who often bring unique experiences and viewpoints to discussions.

However, amidst the concern, some analysts urge a cautious approach to addressing these changes. They suggest that rather than a rapid escalation of pressure, a sustained dialogue could yield better long-term outcomes for Afghan women. “Constructive engagement rather than isolation might be key in influencing a more favorable environment for women’s rights in Afghanistan,” stated Dr. Sarah Ahmad, an expert in international relations.

As a result, while the new policy stands as a significant challenge for women in Afghanistan, ongoing conversations about strategies for advocacy and support are emerging. Various organizations are working on initiatives aimed at amplifying women's voices through alternative channels. Online platforms and underground networks are being explored as avenues for Afghan women to share their thoughts and build solidarity, albeit under risky conditions.
